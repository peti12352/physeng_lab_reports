I  Theoretical summary  1   Introduction  Since its discovery, superconductivity has been an intensively researched field of modern solid state physics.   In general, superconductors (SCs) have two main phenomenological properties, the zero electrical resistance and the perfect diamagnetism (Meissner effect), strikingly different from classical electrodynamics. The latter one is the result of circulating screening current on the surface of the SCs, thereby expelling the magnetic field from the bulk material.   The exotic behavior of the SCs offers such potential applications as the source of high-field electromagnets (magnentic resonance imaging, MRI), magnetic levitation, quantum bits, etc. The microscopic origin of superconductivity is the electron-electron and electron-lattice interaction in the materials; however, its detailed discussion is beyond the scope of this measurement course; we only note that the electrons feel an attractive interaction and they form so-called Cooper pairs as a spin singlet in traditional SCs.   The characteristic binding energy of these pairs is   ∆ , making the quasiparticle spectrum gapped. In this laboratory exercise, the focus will be on the experimental investigation of a so- called high-temperature SC. Contrary to elementary SCs usually requiring as low critical temperature (where the phase transition from the normal to the supeconducting state occurs) as the order of   ≈   1   K, high-temperature SCs built from compounds can enter the superconducting phase even at   ≈   100   K, which can be easily achieved by liquid nitrogen with its boiling point of   ≈   77   K. The combination of SCs with normal metals, semiconductors, or insulators (Is) in nanocircuits is the basic building block in the state-of-the art quantum electronics. In this course, we study Josephson junctions (JJs) and a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) built from them.
